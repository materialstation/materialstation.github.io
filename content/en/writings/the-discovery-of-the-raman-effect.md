The beauty of the blue Mediterranean Sea mesmerized a great scientist and led to the discovery of the Raman effect.

In 1871, Lord Rayleigh analyzed scattered sunlight and concluded that the intensity of the scattered light is inversely proportional to the **[fourth power](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rayleigh_scattering "Source: en.wikipedia.org")** of its wavelength. According to Rayleigh, the color of the Mediterranean Sea was nothing more than a **[reflection](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/On-the-Molecular-Scattering-of-Light-in-Water-and-Raman/1ab684d45c8055599aaaf2ef47d5e8c23b95439b "Source: semanticscholar.org")** of the blue sky.

</figure>

The Indian scientist Chandrasekhara Venkata Raman was not satisfied with Rayleigh’s explanation of the sea’s color. He decided to focus on the phenomenon of light scattering and investigated it further through experiments he performed with the simple equipment he had. In March 1928, the audience at his lecture was stunned to hear the Indian physicist’s discovery.

He simply described his observation as *“a new kind of radiation or light emission from atoms and molecules”*.

  <figcaption>Schematic illustration of the discovery of the Raman effect</figcaption>
</figure>

Raman used sunlight as a high-intensity light source. The white light was collected by a telescope and passed through a violet filter, a monochromatic filter. The filtered light was scattered as it passed through a liquid sample. Most of the scattered light had the same color as the incident violet light; this was later called Rayleigh-scattered light.

However, Raman observed that some of the scattered light had a different color from the Rayleigh-scattered light.

He therefore placed a second (green) filter behind the sample and was able to isolate the green light. After observing the same effect in a series of liquid samples, Raman reported this inelastic scattering as a “secondary radiation” in *Nature*. Reporting the effect took time because the light source was not intense enough: a high-intensity source was needed to observe the weak Raman effect. At the time, the only available source was sunlight, which could be concentrated into a highly intense beam using a **[refracting telescope](https://www.acs.org/content/acs/en/education/whatischemistry/landmarks/ramaneffect.html "Source: acs.org")**.

Over time, the basic components of the instrument were replaced by improved instrumentation.

Various light sources and detection systems were tried until Raman spectroscopy reached its present, state-of-the-art commercial form.
