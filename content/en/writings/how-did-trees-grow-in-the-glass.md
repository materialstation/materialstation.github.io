Seven square pieces of glass in a plastic ice cream box were staring at me proudly, hiding their identity. Of course, we had various Petri dishes in the laboratory, but I preferred to keep them in my favorite ice cream box. It was like sending a secret message to the universe:

*“Rather a bitter truth than a sweet lie.”*

Because I had to discover, in a limited time, what they were made of, and then analyze their defects. The most effective characterization tool for identifying the samples was ***[Raman spectroscopy](https://www.elsevier.com/books/introductory-raman-spectroscopy/ferraro/978-0-12-254105-6 "Source: elsevier.com")***.

When the material is irradiated with a laser, the light scattered from it is filtered, and the light that passes through is dispersed by a diffraction grating so that it can be separated into specific vibrational modes. In Raman spectroscopy, each vibrational mode is measured as a frequency shift from the incident beam. The spectral collection of these characteristic frequencies is considered a unique fingerprint of each molecule. Therefore, the Raman spectrum gives highly useful information for identifying materials and analyzing their structure.

After the Raman analysis, I had to examine the overall condition of the glass defects under an optical microscope to include a detailed comparison in the report. I placed one of the glass samples under the 5X lens of the microscope. (The **[magnification](https://www.auburn.edu/academic/classes/zy/hist0509/html/02basmic.html "Source: auburn.edu")** of an object under the microscope comes from the objective and ocular lenses, so the total magnification of an image equals the objective magnification multiplied by the ocular magnification.) I was able to see the defect inside the glass. It was so lovely and seemed so innocent. However, defects are never desired in glass, since they reduce its strength and quality. I took a closer look at the defect, switched to the 20X lens, and was amazed by what I saw.

**Where did these beautiful shapes come from!?**

I had never seen anything like it before the experiment! The 100X magnification clearly revealed the tree-like shape that had formed inside the defect. I was both excited and curious about the possible source of this beauty. I started to replay the experimental steps in my mind and forced myself to think about what could have caused these forms. However, that was not enough to find the answer, so I researched glass defects and came across *[a study](https://ceramics.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/j.1551-2916.2006.01441.x "Source: wiley.com")* based on a similar observation!

When I focused the laser inside the defect region, the temperature rose quickly there because of the excessive laser exposure, and a low-viscosity melt formed inside the defect. Once I turned the laser off, the temperature in the region dropped, and the cooling led to dendritic crystal growth. That is how I observed the formation of glass trees!

I admit that I damaged the sample during the experiment, but this failure turned into artistic beauty :) Do you agree with me?
