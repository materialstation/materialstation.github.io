**What is the resolution limit of the eye?**

This is a very important question, because we are mostly limited by our senses: sight, hearing, smell, taste and touch. Are these enough to observe the world we live in? We should think hard about this question, because our reality is shaped by these limitations. Of course, microscopes are great helpers when we examine objects we cannot see.

**But what if the eyes of the microscope, its objectives, are limited too?**

The Irish scientist Edward Synge’s mind was most probably busy with this problem, since he knew that imaging beyond these limits could reveal a new world we were not even aware of.

To be able to travel within this new world, he needed to think about the diffraction limit.

**What is the diffraction limit?**

The term “diffraction limit” originates from Abbe’s wave theory. Classical optical microscopy is limited in resolution, and because of the nature of light this limit cannot be overcome with conventional optical microscope lenses. When an object is imaged in the visible range (approximately 0.4 to 0.7 µm), a resolution better than about 250 nm cannot be obtained because of the diffraction limit of light **([see the equation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diffraction-limited_system "Source: en.wikipedia.org"))**.

**Limit, limit, limit… Let’s overcome this limit, but how?**

Synge proposed a fascinating idea to overcome the diffraction limit, and with it the resolution limit, of optical microscopy. The limit can be broken by bringing a nanometric aperture (the metal screen in the image below) very close to the sample surface, into a region called ***[the near-field zone](https://link.springer.com/book/10.1007/978-3-540-37316-2 "Source: link.springer.com")***. The aperture is critical for obtaining high-resolution images, because *high-spatial-frequency electromagnetic fields* are produced at the aperture. Collecting the information from the near-field zone by confining those fields at the aperture probe is the only way to build a high-resolution image. This **[proposal](https://pubs.acs.org/doi/abs/10.1021/acs.chemrev.6b00337 "Source: pubs.acs.org")** was the core idea behind the working principle of ***[Near-Field Scanning Optical Microscopy (SNOM)](https://aip.scitation.org/doi/10.1063/1.481382 "Source: aip.scitation.org")***, which can reveal the optical, structural and chemical properties of matter ***even at length scales of 10 nm!***
