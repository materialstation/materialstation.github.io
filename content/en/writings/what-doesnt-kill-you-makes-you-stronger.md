Anyone who has taken an Introduction to Materials Engineering course will probably understand what I am going to talk about.

In this course, the professor explained how materials behave when they are exposed to different conditions, along with the theoretical background. Even though I found his teaching style quite boring, learning about the behavior of materials was fascinating!

That day he was talking about the ***[mechanisms of strengthening in metals](https://www.wiley.com/en-us/Materials+Science+and+Engineering%3A+An+Introduction%2C+10th+Edition-p-9781119405498 "Source: wiley.com")***. *Grain-size reduction, solid-solution alloying and strain hardening* can be counted as ways of strengthening single-phase metals. The main purpose of applying these methods is to modify and enhance the properties of materials, such as hardness. *[Hardness](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hardness "Source: en.wikipedia.org")* is defined as the resistance to surface deformation (localized plastic deformation) and the ability to withstand scratching caused by mechanical effects.

The most famous and well-known metal is steel, so he first focused on the hardening mechanism in carbon steels. The type of steel is named according to its carbon and alloying element content. The type of steel and the hardening mechanism he mentioned were martensitic steel and ***transformation hardening***, respectively. *[Martensite](https://material-properties.org/what-is-transformation-hardening-martensitic-transformation-hardening-definition/ "Source: material-properties.org")* is formed by rapid cooling from austenite, an iron–carbon phase that can exist above 723 °C and below about 1500 °C, depending on the carbon content. In this transformation, quenching takes place at *[such a high rate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Martensite "Source: en.wikipedia.org")* that carbon atoms do not have time to diffuse out of the crystal structure. The change in microstructure therefore results in a supersaturated, very hard crystalline form of steel. Rapid, harsh cooling locks the atoms in place, and that is exactly what makes the steel so hard.

While the professor was emphasizing the effects of severe conditions on materials during the transformation process, I was busy writing the following sentences in my notebook:

*No need to do an experiment to understand the logic of transformation hardening in metals.*

*Everyone and everything changes and becomes stronger when exposed to severe conditions.*

Years later, whenever life puts me through a sudden “quench”, I remember those two sentences in my notebook.
