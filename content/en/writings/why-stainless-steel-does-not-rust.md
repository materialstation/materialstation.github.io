Leave an ordinary steel nail outside for a few weeks and it turns orange. Leave a stainless steel spoon out for years and it barely changes. Both are mostly iron — so what's the difference?

## The secret ingredient: chromium

Stainless steel contains at least **10.5% chromium**. Chromium reacts with oxygen even faster than iron does, and the oxide it forms behaves very differently from rust.

Rust is flaky and porous. It peels away and exposes fresh metal underneath, so corrosion keeps going. Chromium oxide, by contrast, forms a **dense, continuous film only a few nanometres thick** — tightly bonded to the surface and invisible to the eye. Engineers call this the *passive layer*.

## A shield that repairs itself

The remarkable part is what happens when you scratch it. As long as oxygen is around, the exposed chromium immediately reacts and the film grows back. The steel heals its own armour.

## Not invincible

Stainless steel *can* corrode. Chloride ions — from seawater, road salt, or some cleaning products — can puncture the passive film at tiny spots, creating deep pits. That's why marine hardware often uses grade **316**, which adds molybdenum to strengthen the film, rather than the common kitchen grade **304**.

So the next time you wash a stainless pan, remember: you're looking at a nanotechnology that's been protecting us for over a century.
