It was a sweltering day, and I could not find the energy to get out of bed. I was waiting for anything that would tempt me to get ready for a new day.

It could have been the smell of delicious food, noisy neighbors or the endless questions in my head. Unfortunately, none of them had the power to push me out of bed, except one thing!

My bedroom had a balcony that my family mostly used as a cellar. My mom had probably opened the balcony door while I was sleeping and left it open to air the room. Sunshine was falling on the floor through the half-open door, and the reflected sunlight seemed to slide gently across the floor. So, who was bending the sunlight like that?

Intuitively, I knew it had to be a solid material that reflects light easily.

**What was that material?**

I got out of bed curiously and walked towards the half-open door. I did not want to push the door and sweep the reflected light away, so I brought my right cheek close to the door and peeked into the balcony with my left eye. It was a bowl, forgotten in dust and dirt, that had somehow found a way to remind us of itself. I quickly picked it up and turned it in my palm to see its full beauty. That is when I noticed the special mark that time had left on the copper bowl: the inevitable consequence of not taking care of a metal bowl.

I knew the reason for the green tears of this copper bowl. We are familiar with these tears because copper has had a place in many applications throughout human history. For example, it was used for kitchenware **[in Anatolia](https://www.anadoluuygarliklari.com/hititler/hititlerde-madencilik/ "Source: anadoluuygarliklari.com")**, **[preferred](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0167572915000217 "Source: sciencedirect.com")** for pipes in ancient Egypt, and it is built into the electronic devices of the modern world.

The greenish tears start with oxidation. The copper surface first forms a thin oxide layer, which slowly reacts with moisture and with carbon dioxide or sulfur compounds in the air to form the green copper carbonates and sulfates known as **[patina](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Patina "Source: en.wikipedia.org")**. When a metal surface is in contact with an oxygen-rich environment (our balcony, in this case), an oxide can grow on it. Reactions such as ***[oxygen chemisorption, nucleation and growth of the surface oxide layer, and bulk oxide growth](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0167572915000217 "Source: sciencedirect.com")*** are accepted as the sources of oxidation on a clean metal surface. This phenomenon can be observed in both macro- and nano-sized copper materials. Unfortunately, it is exposure to harmful chemicals such as ammonium sulfate, together with oxygen, that produces the beautiful greenish colors of copper’s tears.

The good news is that copper does not break down at the molecular level because of oxidation. Instead, it can form ***[a protective layer](https://rotaxmetals.net/how-to-oxidize-copper/ "Source: rotaxmetals.net")*** that keeps the core of the material from further damage.

To make the long story short, “Behind every beautiful thing, there’s been some kind of pain “
- Bob Dylan.
