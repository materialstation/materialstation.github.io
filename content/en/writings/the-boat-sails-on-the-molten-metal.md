The idea of coating materials has always sounded interesting to me. Your source material may somehow turn into something very valuable or into a piece of rubbish. People are willing to pay more for something coated with gold or other valuable metals, so researchers do their best to keep people happy by improving the quality of the coating. But there is a very important factor behind this success: a boat!

Yes, but this boat sails on molten metal rather than on a river. How come?

Metal can be deposited, or coated, onto a substrate by the **[thermal evaporation](https://www.elsevier.com/books/magnetism-of-surfaces-interfaces-and-nanoscale-materials/camley/978-0-444-62634-9 "Source: elsevier.com")** method. In this method, the source material you want to coat with is placed inside the boat and heated by resistive heating electrodes, so the evaporation rate can be adjusted through the current supplied inside the vacuum chamber. However, the method has some disadvantages, and one of them also explains why my boat sank, or rather broke, after the evaporation process. If the boat material does not have a much higher melting temperature than the source material it carries, the boat breaks apart because of the excessive heat applied to melt the source. The current should also be applied at a rate that does not create temperature differences across the boat body.

Luckily, I was able to deposit some chromium on a silicon substrate, but I had to throw the boat into a recycling bin at the end of the process.
